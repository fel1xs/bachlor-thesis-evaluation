## Seminar Content and Learning Objectives

The seminar _“Topics in International Economic Policy”_ provides an in-depth examination and discussion of current issues in international economic policy. Through the intensive written analysis of contemporary questions in this field, students are enabled to independently develop economic policy solutions based on their understanding of economic relationships. Building on this, they are able to derive well-founded academic judgments on complex issues in international economic policy, defend these arguments, and contribute to related topics within the context of academic discussion. The module assessment is based on a 30-minute presentation on the draft of the written paper, as well as the corresponding final written paper.

## Navigating Uncertainty: Historical Lessons for International Economic Policy

This seminar examines how past episodes of economic uncertainty can inform current debates in international economic policy. Drawing on examples ranging from the Great Depression and post-war reconstruction to recent crises and technological change, the course analyzes how governments and central banks have responded to shocks, instability, and structural transformation.

Students will select one topic and develop a seminar paper that engages with both historical evidence and current policy debates. They are expected to incorporate recent contributions from the economic literature, apply them to policy-relevant questions, and derive well-founded policy implications. The objective is to evaluate the extent to which past experiences can inform present-day policy choices, while recognizing the limitations of historical analogies.

At the end of the course, students will present their findings, enabling comparison across topics and approaches. The seminar aims to strengthen participants’ understanding of international economic policy, institutional frameworks, and the trade-offs policymakers face under conditions of uncertainty.

## Seminar Topics

|  | Topic |
| --- | --- |
| **1.** | **The New Deal versus NGEU: Fiscal Centralization and Common Debt**<br>Both the 1930s New Deal and the 2020 NextGenerationEU (NGEU) represent shifts towards centralized fiscal policy and common-debt financing in response to systemic shocks. Both programs involved uneven regional development and prompted discussions over who pays and who benefits. How do the experiences with fiscal centralization and common-debt of the 1930s New Deal help understand the economic and political constraints of NextGenerationEU?<br>Benchmark reference: Fishback, P. V. (2017). “How Successful Was the New Deal? The Microeconomic Impact of New Deal Spending and Lending Policies in the 1930s.” Journal of Economic Literature. |
| **2.** | **From Smoot–Hawley to a Revival of Protectionism**<br>The Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930 triggered retaliatory protectionism and prolonged the Great Depression through disrupted supply chains and heightened uncertainty. How do the welfare losses and international reactions triggered by the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act compare to the macroeconomic consequences of contemporary protectionist policies like the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) or the US Liberation Day tariffs?<br>Benchmark   reference: Eichengreen, B. (1986). “The Political Economy of the Smoot-Hawley Tariff.” NBER Working Paper No. 2001. National Bureau of Economic Research. |
| **3.** | **Latin American Import Substitution and Modern Industrial Policy**<br>From the 1950s to the 1970s, many Latin American countries pursued import substitution industrialization (ISI), using tariffs, quotas, and state-led investment to promote domestic manufacturing and reduce dependence on imports. While it initially supported industrial growth, it often resulted in inefficiencies, limited export competitiveness, and rent seeking. Today, industrial policy has re-emerged in advanced economies through policies aimed at reshoring production, protecting strategic sectors, and reducing dependence on global supply chains. To what extent do the successes and failures of ISI provide lessons for the design of modern industrial policy?<br>Réka Juhász, Nathan Lane, Dani Rodrik. 2024. The New Economics of Industrial Policy. Annual Review Economics. 16:213-242. |
| **4.** | **The Legacy of the Bretton Woods System**<br>The Nixon Shock ended dollar-gold convertibility, ushering in floating exchange rates and higher exchange rate volatility. The episode illustrates time inconsistency in international monetary arrangements and illustrates the inherent instability of asymmetrical monetary systems. What lessons can the collapse of the Bretton Woods system provide for the euro area or the role of the Euro in a global financial system that is still dominated by the dollar?<br>Benchmark reference: Farese, G., Guerrieri, P., and Padoan, P. C. (2023). “European Monetary Regimes after the Fall of Bretton Woods: A Political Economy Approach.” Oxford Review of Economic Policy. |
| **5.** | **1970s Oil Shocks vs Contemporary Stagflation Risks**<br>The 1973 and 1979 oil crises triggered stagflation and challenged the Phillips curve assumption. These events highlighted knowledge problems in managing exogenous price shocks. Policy shifts toward monetarism and supply-side reforms followed. Does the macroeconomic transmission of the 1970s oil price shocks differ from the inflationary spikes of the early 2020s, and what does this imply for an optimal monetary policy response?<br>Benchmark reference: Reis, R. (2022). “The Burst of High Inflation in 2021–22: How and Why Did We Get Here?” BIS Working Paper No. 1060. Bank for International Settlements. |
| **6.** | **Computerization and Labor Market Policy**<br>The diffusion of computers from the 1970s onward led to labor reallocation across sectors and occupations, but the adjustment process differed across institutional settings. The US model offered flexibility and allowed for rapid reallocation, while the German model relied more on coordination, training, and employment protection. To what extent can differences in labor market institutions and policy responses explain different labor market outcomes, and what lessons do they offer for managing AI-driven labor market change?<br>Benchmark reference: Antonczyk, D., DeLeire, T., & Fitzenberger, B. (2018). Polarization and rising wage inequality: Comparing the U.S. and Germany. Econometrics, 6(2), 20. |
| **7.** | **The 1980s Latin American Debt Crisis and the Modern "Sudden Stop" Risk**<br>The 1980s debt crisis, triggered by surging US interest rates and commodity busts, serves as a stark warning for today's highly indebted emerging markets and peripheral European economies facing tighter monetary conditions. To what extent can the "sudden stop" dynamics and sovereign default risks of the 1980s Latin American debt crisis help explain the fiscal vulnerabilities of highly indebted European governments in an environment of normalizing interest rates?<br>Benchmark reference: Calvo, G. A., Izquierdo, A., and Mejía, L.-F. (2008). “Systemic Sudden Stops: The Relevance of Balance-Sheet Effects and Financial Integration.” NBER Working Paper No. 14026. National Bureau of Economic Research. |
| **8.** | **The ERM Crisis (1992-1993) and the Fragility of the Eurozone**<br>The ERM crises of the early 1990s illustrated how speculative attacks, driven by self-fulfilling market expectations, can force the collapse of fixed exchange rate regimes. To what extent do lessons from the speculative attacks of the 1992 ERM crisis help assess whether the euro area can prevent sovereign debt crises today, and what are the short- and long-run trade-offs of anti-fragmentation policies?<br>Benchmark reference: De Grauwe, P., and Ji, Y. (2022). “The Fragility of the Eurozone: Has It Disappeared?” Journal of International Money and Finance. |
| **9.** | **The Dot-Com Bubble and AI**<br>The crash of the Dot-Com bubble provides a case study of asset bubbles that may be driven by a combination of technological uncertainty and loose monetary policies. What parallels exist between the central bank policy responses to the Dot-Com bubble and the current management of asset price inflation driven by Artificial Intelligence technologies?<br>Pástor, Ľ., & Veronesi, P. (2006). Was there a Nasdaq bubble in the late 1990s? Journal of Financial Economics, 81(1), 61–100. |
| **10.** | **The Eurozone Sovereign Debt Crisis and the New Fiscal Rules**<br>The Eurozone crisis exposed structural weaknesses in the fiscal framework of the monetary union and prompted significant reforms. To what extent do these reforms reduce the risk of similar crises, do the revised EU fiscal rules address underlying vulnerabilities, and how robust are they under conditions of heightened economic uncertainty?<br>Benchmark reference: Blanchard, O., Leandro, A. & Zettelmeyer, J. 2021. "Redesigning EU fiscal rules: from rules to standards," CEPR, CESifo. |
